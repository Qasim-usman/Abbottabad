    2. Food

    Eat like you came up the hill

    Hazara cooking is meat, tomato, ghee and restraint — big flavours from short ingredient lists, cooked hot and fast in a heavy pan. Add cold-water trout, pink tea and a bazaar full of honey and walnuts, and you have a very good three days of eating.

    Order these first

    The dishes that matter

    Six things to eat and drink before you leave. None of them are hard to find; most places on the main roads do at least three of them well.

    Signature

    Chapli kabab

    A wide, flat patty of minced beef mixed with tomato, coriander, crushed pomegranate seeds and gram flour, pressed thin and shallow-fried on a big steel griddle. Crisp at the frilly edges, soft in the middle, eaten torn into naan with raw onion. The name comes from how flat it is — and a proper one is nearly the size of a plate.

    images/chicken-karahi.jpg
    Main

    Hazara-style chicken karahi

    Chicken, tomatoes, green chilli, ginger and a lot of ghee, cooked hard in a karahi until the sauce tightens around the meat. There is no onion paste and very little masala — the point is the tomato and the fat. Order it with fresh naan and do not expect cutlery to be much use.

    Main

    Dumba & mutton karahi

    Dumba is fat-tailed sheep, and its meat is the richer, more prized cousin of ordinary mutton karahi — sweeter, softer, cooked in its own fat. It is a dish for a group and a long evening. Ask what is fresh that day; good places will tell you honestly.

    Cold water, hot tea

    The hill specialities

    What the altitude gives you: fish from cold streams, and two kinds of tea that are worth crossing town for.

    Main

    Hill-stream trout

    Trout thrives in the cold water of the Galyat and the valleys north of here, and it is usually served the right way: whole, salted, fried or grilled, with lemon and nothing else. Restaurants on the Nathiagali and Thandiani roads do it best. Ask the price by weight before you order — it is charged per kilo and it is not the cheap option.

    Drink

    Kashmiri pink chai

    Green tea leaves cooked long with a pinch of soda until the liquor turns deep, then whisked with milk until it goes pink, and finished with crushed pistachios and almonds. Rich, faintly savoury, served sweet here. It appears at weddings, in winter, and in any café that wants to be taken seriously.

    Drink

    Doodh patti

    Tea boiled entirely in milk with no water at all, sweetened hard and poured from a height. It is the default fuel of every bazaar, office and hostel in the city, costs very little, and is the thing you will be handed within two minutes of anyone finding out you are a guest.

    Buy on the way home

    Straight off the hillside

    Bring home

    Local honey

    Sold along the roads out of the city in reused bottles and proper jars alike, from acacia-light to almost black. Taste before you buy where you are offered the chance, and expect to pay more for the dark, thick stuff from higher up.

    Bring home

    Walnuts, dried apricots & pine nuts

    Hazara walnuts are small, oily and worth cracking yourself. Dried apricots and pine nuts come down from the north and are sold by the kilo in the bazaars — a paper bag of them is the standard thing to take back to relatives.

    Street food

    Roasted corn at Shimla Hill

    Whole cobs blackened over coals, rubbed with salt, chilli and lemon, handed over in a twist of paper. Eaten standing at the top of Shimla Hill with the sun going down, it is the single most Abbottabad thing on this page.

    Where it happens

    Eating streets & bazaars

    Abbottabad does not really have a restaurant district. It has roads that fill up with food in the evening, and bazaars that never stopped.

    Road

    Mansehra Road

    The main artery north, and where most of the newer, larger restaurants are: karahi houses, barbecue, fast food, cafés with parking. If you are eating as a family or a group and want a table without hunting, start here.

    Road

    Jinnah Road (The Mall)

    The old central spine through town. Sweet shops, bakeries, juice counters, tea stalls and small kabab places, mixed in with everything else the city sells. Best walked in the early evening.

    Bazaar

    Kachehri Bazaar

    Older, denser and louder, near the courts. Chai in glasses, fried snacks, dried fruit, spices sold from open sacks. This is where to go if you want the bazaar itself rather than a menu.

    One day, seven stops

    Must-try in one day

    A route that works as written: breakfast in the bazaar, a long lunch, tea in the afternoon, corn at sunset and karahi after dark. Walk the middle of it.

    1. 08:30 · Halwa puri in the bazaar Start heavy, the way the city does: fried puri, chickpeas and a scoop of sweet halwa at an old shop off Jinnah Road, with a glass of doodh patti to follow.
    2. 10:30 · Chai and a walk through Kachehri Bazaar Let breakfast settle while you look at the dried-fruit sacks and the spice stalls. Buy walnuts now, before you are carrying anything else.
    3. 13:00 · Chapli kabab, hot off the griddle Find a place with a griddle out front and a queue at it. One kabab with naan and raw onion is a light lunch; two is a commitment.
    4. 15:30 · Kashmiri pink chai The afternoon reset. Order it with nuts on top and sit somewhere you can watch the road.
    5. 17:30 · Roasted corn at Shimla Hill Walk up for the view, buy a cob from the coals at the top, and stay for the sunset over the valley.
    6. 19:30 · Karahi on Mansehra Road Chicken if it is just you, dumba if there are four of you. Fresh naan, green chilli, and no cutlery.
    7. 21:30 · Kheer, rabri or a plate of sweets Finish at a sweet shop on the way back. Take a box of gulab jamun to whoever you are staying with; it is expected.

    If you are only eating one meal here, make it karahi with a group. If you only have twenty minutes, make it chapli kabab.

    A few practical notes

    Food here is generous rather than delicate, and portions are built for sharing — a single karahi will feed two people comfortably and three at a push. Menus rarely list prices for meat dishes by portion; trout and dumba are sold by weight, so agree the quantity before it goes in the pan. Vegetarians are not badly served, but you will need to ask specifically: daal, saag in season, chana, mixed vegetable, and every bread on the list are all easy to find, while a "vegetable" karahi may still be cooked in a pan that has seen meat all day.

    Stick to bottled or filtered water, especially outside the city, and be a little careful with cut fruit and ice from small stalls in high summer. Tea is safe everywhere because it is boiled. Ramadan changes the day completely: most places close through daylight hours and then open in force at sunset, which is one of the best times of year to eat here if you can adjust your clock. Prices in this guide are deliberately not quoted — they move too fast to be useful — but street food remains very cheap, and a full sit-down meal is still modest by big-city standards.

    Eaten well?
    Now meet the people.

    Hindko, hospitality, festivals and the Hazarewal habit of feeding guests first and asking questions later.
