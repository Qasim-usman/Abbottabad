    A valley with a very long memory

    Abbottabad itself is young — it was laid out in January 1853 — but the valley it sits in has been a corridor for traders, pilgrims, armies and empires for well over two thousand years. Here is the long version, from Gandhara to the city you can walk around today.

    The short answer

    Geography wrote the first draft

    Abbottabad sits in a bowl at roughly 1,260 metres, ringed by pine ridges, with the Sarban and Shimla hills on either side. That bowl happens to lie on the natural route between the plains of the Punjab and the mountains of the north — the way you go if you are heading for Kashmir, Gilgit or, eventually, China. Almost everything that has happened here follows from that one fact.

    Because it was a corridor, the valley collected people. Because it was high and cool, whoever controlled it wanted to stay in summer. And because it had space and water, the British turned it into a garrison — which is why a city of schools, colleges and cantonment roads grew where a scatter of villages used to be.

    From Gandhara to now

    Dates before the nineteenth century are deliberately approximate — the valley's early history is read from rocks, coins and monasteries rather than registers.

    1. c. 500 BCE onwards

      Gandhara, and the road north

      The Hazara valley sat on the eastern edge of Gandhara, the ancient region centred on the Peshawar and Taxila basins. Taxila — a great city of the era — is only a couple of hours away by today's roads, and the tracks that linked it to Kashmir and the upper Indus ran through these hills. Persians, Greeks after Alexander, Mauryans, Indo-Greeks, Scythians and Kushans all held or passed through the wider region, and the valley absorbed a little of each.

    2. 3rd century BCE – 5th century CE

      Rock edicts and monasteries

      Buddhism took deep root in Gandhara, and the clearest local trace is the set of Ashokan rock edicts at Mansehra, roughly 25 km up the road from Abbottabad — an emperor's instructions on tolerance and non-violence, carved into boulders in the third century BCE and still legible. Stupas and monastery sites are scattered across the Hazara hills. If you want the fullest picture of that world, the museums at Taxila and Peshawar hold what was dug out of it.

    3. 8th – 18th century

      Kashmiri, Turkic, Mughal and Durrani hands

      Over the long middle stretch the valley changed owners repeatedly: Kashmiri and Hindu Shahi rulers, then Turkic and Afghan dynasties, then the Mughals — for whom Hazara was a minor but useful strip on the Kashmir road — and finally the Durrani Afghans from the mid-eighteenth century. Through all of it, day-to-day power sat with local tribes and clans in the side valleys rather than with whoever claimed the territory on a map.

    4. 1820s – 1849

      The Sikh decades

      Ranjit Singh's Sikh Empire pushed into Hazara in the 1820s, and his commander Hari Singh Nalwa became the region's best-remembered — and most feared — governor. He founded Haripur, about 40 km south-west of present-day Abbottabad, in 1822: the first properly planned town in the valley, with a fort, a bazaar and a water channel. Sikh rule was resisted hard in the hills, and the fighting of these decades shaped how the next set of rulers approached the place.

    5. 1849

      Hazara becomes British

      After the Second Anglo-Sikh War the British annexed the Punjab, and Hazara came with it. A young officer named James Abbott, who had already spent years in the region on political missions, was made the district's first Deputy Commissioner. His job was to pacify a hill district, collect revenue from clans who had just spent thirty years fighting the last administration, and find a permanent base from which to do both.

    6. January 1853

      The city is founded

      In January 1853 Abbott established a new headquarters town in the bowl below Sarban hill, and it was named Abbottabad after him — abad, the common suffix across the subcontinent, simply meaning a settled or inhabited place. He did not stay long: he left the district within a few months. He is also remembered for a wistful poem about the valley he was leaving, which is quoted locally far more often than anything else he wrote.

    7. 1853 – 1947

      Garrison, hill station, district town

      Under British rule Abbottabad grew into the administrative centre of Hazara district and a substantial cantonment — home for decades to Gurkha and frontier regiments. The cool climate did the rest: wide tree-lined roads, bungalows with gardens, a club, a church. St Luke's Church, built in the 1860s, still stands on the cantonment side of town. Roads were pushed up into the Galyat so that Murree, Nathiagali and Dunga Gali became summer retreats, and the town became the place you stopped on the way to them.

    8. 1947 – 1950s

      Pakistan, and a military academy

      Partition in 1947 brought a new country, new arrivals and a new role. The Pakistan Military Academy was established at Kakul, on the edge of the city, and began training officers from early 1948 — every commissioned officer in the Pakistan Army since has passed through it. Burn Hall, a missionary school relocated from Srinagar, reopened here and was later taken over by the army as Army Burn Hall College. The pattern of the modern city — cantonment, academy, schools — was set within a decade.

    9. Late 1970s

      The Karakoram Highway arrives

      The Karakoram Highway — built jointly by Pakistan and China over roughly two decades and completed in the late 1970s — runs from the Punjab up the Indus to the Khunjerab Pass on the Chinese border, and its lower stretch passes right by Abbottabad. Overnight the city became a staging post for the entire north: freight, traders, climbers, students and tourists all funnelled through it. Ayub Medical College and its teaching hospital opened in the same era, making Abbottabad the referral centre for a very large area of hill country.

    10. 1980s – 2000s

      A city that runs on schools

      If Abbottabad has a reputation inside Pakistan, this is it: people send their children here to study. Alongside the PMA and Army Burn Hall came Ayub Medical Complex, a COMSATS university campus around the turn of the millennium, an agriculture university campus, cadet colleges, nursing schools and a dense layer of private academies and hostels. In term time the bazaars are full of students, and a remarkable share of the local economy is education, healthcare and the rented rooms that go with them.

    11. 8 October 2005

      The Kashmir earthquake

      On the morning of 8 October 2005 a magnitude 7.6 earthquake struck with its epicentre near Muzaffarabad. The devastation was worst in Azad Kashmir and in Balakot, about 60 km north of here, which was effectively destroyed. Abbottabad was shaken hard and lost buildings and lives of its own, but it stayed standing — and so became one of the main staging grounds for the relief effort, its hospitals, schools and open ground absorbing casualties and displaced families from the valleys above for months afterwards. Rebuilding across the region ran for years, and it changed how people here think about construction.

    12. Today

      Abbottabad now

      The city has a couple of hundred thousand residents and the surrounding district well over a million, and it has grown up the hillsides in a way the cantonment planners never imagined. The Hazara Motorway (E-35) has cut the drive from Islamabad to roughly two hours, which has brought weekend traffic, new cafés and a lot more construction. What has not changed is the reason people come: the pines, the ridge lines, the cold water, and a valley that still feels like the doorway to the mountains.

    Where to see it for yourself

    1860s

    St Luke's Church

    A small stone church from the early cantonment years, still in use, on a quiet road among old trees.

    Cantonment

    The old roads

    The grid of wide, tree-lined cantonment avenues is the original town plan, more or less untouched.

    3rd c. BCE

    Mansehra rock edicts

    Ashoka's carved instructions on boulders, about 25 km north — the valley's oldest writing.

    Haripur
